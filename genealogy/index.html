I · The line of descent

Advisor to advisor

  1. I Nicolaus CopernicusDoctorate in canon law, Ferrara · 1503

    Studied Bologna, Padua, Kraków.

    “De revolutionibus orbium coelestium” · displaced the Earth from the centre of the cosmos.

    His own teachers, Leonhard von Dobschütz and Domenico Maria Novara, lie beyond this chart.

  2. II Georg Joachim RheticusMagister artium, Universität Wittenberg · 1535

    The sole pupil of Copernicus; he persuaded his master to publish, and saw the book to press.

    Second teacher: Johannes Volmar (Wittenberg 1515); that line ends one step later.

  3. III Moritz Valentin SteinmetzMagister artium and Dr. med., Universität Leipzig · 1550, 1567

    “De Peste Capita Disputationis Ordinariae”

    Second teacher: Johann Hoffmann — advisor unknown; the line ends there.

  4. IV Christoph MeurerMagister artium, Universität Leipzig · 1582

    “De Iride seu Arcu coelesti” · on the rainbow.

  5. V Philipp MüllerMagister artium, Universität Leipzig · 1604

    Professor of mathematics at Leipzig.

  6. VI Erhard WeigelDr. phil., Universität Leipzig · 1650

    “De ascensionibus et descensionibus astronomicis dissertatio”

    Taught the young Leibniz at Jena.

  7. VII Gottfried Wilhelm LeibnizDr. jur., Universität Altdorf · 1666

    “Disputatio Inauguralis de Casibus Perplexis in Jure” · co-inventor of the calculus.

    Jakob Thomasius · Magister, Universität Leipzig · 1643.

    Bartholomäus Schwendendörffer · Universität Leipzig.

    Christiaan Huygens · Académie royale des sciences, Paris · 1676.

    The Mathematics Genealogy Project records four teachers for Leibniz. Only Weigel’s line, above, climbs onto Copernicus.

  8. VIII Nicolas MalebrancheParis · philosopher and mathematician

    The line divides here, and does not meet again until Poisson.

  9. The Basel line · via Lagrange

    1. IX Jacob BernoulliUniversität Basel · 1684

      “Solutionem tergemini problematis arithmetici, geometrici et astronomici”

    2. X Johann BernoulliDr. med., Universität Basel · 1694

      “Dissertatio Inauguralis Physico-Anatomica de Motu Musculorum”

    3. XI Leonhard EulerUniversität Basel · 1726

      “Dissertatio physica de sono”

    4. XII Joseph-Louis LagrangeNo formal degree · Turin and Berlin

      Taught by Euler through their correspondence.

    The Paris line · via Laplace

    1. IX Pierre VarignonCollège Mazarin, Paris · 1687

      Statics and the mechanics of forces.

    2. X Léonor CaronCollège Mazarin, Paris · 1710

      Taught from Varignon’s own lecture notes.

    3. XI Jean Le Rond d’AlembertCollège Mazarin, Paris · 1735

      The wave equation; co-editor of the Encyclopédie.

    4. XII Pierre-Simon LaplaceParis · 1769

      Celestial mechanics; analytic probability.

  10. XIII Siméon Denis PoissonÉcole Polytechnique, Paris · 1800

    Student of both Lagrange and Laplace; the two lines reunite here.

    Jean-Baptiste Joseph Fourier · Ph.D.

    Gustav P. Lejeune Dirichlet · Hon., Universität Bonn · 1827.

    Rudolf O. S. Lipschitz · Dr. phil., Universität Berlin · 1853.

    Fourier, Dirichlet and Lipschitz were Poisson’s students — relatives of the line, not steps within it.

  11. XIV Michel ChaslesÉcole Polytechnique, Paris · 1814

    Founder of modern projective geometry.

  12. XV Gaston DarbouxPh.D., École Normale Supérieure, Paris · 1866

    “Sur les surfaces orthogonales”

  13. XVI C. Émile (Charles) PicardPh.D., École Normale Supérieure, Paris · 1877

    “Applications des complexes linéaires à l’étude des surfaces et des courbes gauches”

  14. XVII Philippe Emmanuel Le CorbeillerPh.D., Université de Paris · 1926

    “Contribution à l’étude des formes quadratiques à indéterminées conjuguées”

  15. XVIII Mischa SchwartzPh.D., Harvard University · 1951

    “A Statistical Approach to the Automatic Search Problem”

  16. XIX Ness Bomi ShroffPh.D., Columbia University · October 1994

    “Traffic Modeling and Analysis in High-Speed ATM Networks”
